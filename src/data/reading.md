---
books:
  - title: "The Kite Runner"
    author: "Khaled Hosseini"
    note: |
      I didn't like The Kite Runner all that much initially, but I've found it surprisingly difficult to forget. I still remember the moment of standing up for a friend, the hurt, and the pall of sadness that hangs over the story. Those are the things that have stayed with me long after reading it, even as some of the details have faded.

  - title: "Through the Broken Glass: An Autobiography"
    author: "T. N. Seshan"
    note: |
      I've always looked up to T. N. Seshan, India's 10th Chief Election Commissioner, and reading about his life only added to that admiration. The man was an absolute boss, with the idealism, moral conviction and sheer nerve to enforce election rules on politicians who were very used to getting their way. What stays with me is how seriously he took his duty, his sheer honesty and directness, and how willing he was to make powerful people uncomfortable. A book I'd recommend to anyone wondering how to stand their ground when the system they're working within seems determined to wear them down.

  - title: "I Too Had a Dream"
    author: "Verghese Kurien"
    note: |
      This appealed to me for much the same reason as Seshan's autobiography. A young, ambitious man ends up working at a dairy in Anand, somewhere he hadn't imagined building his life, and through great friendships and a dogged determination to make things work, helps build Amul into what we know today. The scale of what followed, helping turn a milk-deficient India into a dairy powerhouse, makes those beginnings even more remarkable. I love reading about people who find themselves somewhere unexpected and end up caring enough to change it.

  - title: "A Thousand Splendid Suns"
    author: "Khaled Hosseini"
    note: |
      Who can forget the man at the bus station, or the scene with the broken teeth? The cruelty in this book is difficult to shake, and the way the two women's stories converge made it even harder for me to forget. After The Kite Runner, I was surprised Hosseini could follow it with another book that stayed with me this much. Back-to-back epics.

  - title: "Vikram Sarabhai: A Life"
    author: "Amrita Shah"
    note: |
      I loved how this book showed that being born into wealth doesn't necessarily quieten the desire to make something of yourself. Reading about Sarabhai and the people around him gave me a sense of the ambition behind so many of India's scientific institutions, and the incredible odds they took on because they wanted a newly independent nation to do great things. The friendships and connections between all these minds were as fascinating to me as the institutions they built.

  - title: "Why I Am an Atheist"
    author: "Bhagat Singh"

  - title: "Robert Langdon series"
    author: "Dan Brown"
    note: |
      I really enjoyed the early books, especially The Da Vinci Code and Angels & Demons, but after the third book the series started feeling too preachy, implausible and repetitive for me. The things that made those first stories exciting became a little too familiar as I kept reading.

  - title: "Animal Farm"
    author: "George Orwell"
    note: |
      An all-time favourite, and my introduction to thinking about power dynamics, political manipulation and how easily people can become unquestioning followers. It gave me a way to recognise those patterns, and I still find myself thinking about it when I see people repeating whatever their leaders tell them.

  - title: "Dollar Bahu"
    author: "Sudha Murty"
    note: |
      A small-town tale that I enjoyed as a casual read, with a familiar feel that reminded me of How I Taught My Grandmother to Read. Something I'd happily pick up when I want to settle into a simple story for a while.

  - title: "1984"
    author: "George Orwell"
    note: |
      Another all-time favourite. The state surveillance is disturbing enough, but what really stays with me is how people can be pushed into accepting control over their lives and even their understanding of reality. It makes me think about how readily we can agree to things without questioning them or understanding the consequences, especially when we've stopped thinking critically.

  - title: "To Kill a Mockingbird"
    author: "Harper Lee"
    note: |
      So, so good. I loved the small-town atmosphere and how clearly it painted a picture of the place and its time, even while dealing with something so painful. The scene where Scout talks to a man in the mob and interrupts its cruelty has stayed with me in particular. There is something about seeing all of this through a child's eyes that makes it hit even harder.

  - title: "Black Beauty"
    author: "Anna Sewell"
    note: |
      I remembered a chapter from our fourth-grade English textbook and eventually read the whole book. It has stayed with me as a comforting read, with that extra bit of affection that comes from finding something you first encountered as a child.

  - title: "The White Tiger"
    author: "Aravind Adiga"
    note: |
      A wonderfully strange, darkly funny take on the class divide, written with a bluntness that kept shocking me. I loved the almost whimsical way the story unfolds while dealing with something so ugly.

  - title: "Kafka on the Shore"
    author: "Haruki Murakami"
    note: |
      This was just too absurd for my taste, and I struggled to find much to connect with. I know people love Murakami, but this one didn't help me understand the hype.

  - title: "Norwegian Wood"
    author: "Haruki Murakami"
    note: |
      I found the coming-of-age story much easier to get into than Kafka on the Shore, and it was something I could actually sit down and read. I still didn't quite come away understanding the Murakami hype, though.

  - title: "Never Logged Out: How the Internet Created India's Gen Z"
    author: "Ria Chopra"
    note: |
      A great look at Gen Z's language and how we live with social media. I liked seeing the terms, habits and ways of communicating taken seriously, because the internet is such a large part of how our generation experiences the world. This is our medium, and there was a lot here that felt familiar.

  - title: "Train to Pakistan"
    author: "Khushwant Singh"
    note: |
      A fantastic story set during the Partition of India and Pakistan. I found a lot of charm in the village and the way its people were written, which made the violence surrounding them all the more painful to read about.

  - title: "Days at the Morisaki Bookshop"
    author: "Satoshi Yagisawa"
    note: |
      A lovely, feel-good read that has the easy rhythm of someone telling you about their life. I really enjoyed the slice-of-life feel of it, and it's the sort of book I'd happily recommend for an idle afternoon.

  - title: "More Days at the Morisaki Bookshop"
    author: "Satoshi Yagisawa"
    note: |
      More of that cosy, slice-of-life feeling I enjoyed in the first book. Both of these made for lovely, unhurried reading, and I don't think you'll regret spending a quiet afternoon with them.

  - title: "The Palace of Illusions"
    author: "Chitra Banerjee Divakaruni"
    note: |
      Amazing, amazing, amazing. A wonderfully poignant retelling of the Mahabharata from Draupadi's perspective, and one that made the story feel deeply personal. I loved the twists and the way it drew me into her life. Definitely read this one.

  - title: "The Mussoorie Murders"
    author: "Divyaroop Bhatnagar"
    note: |
      This felt like an Indian attempt at a Feluda-meets-Poirot mystery, and I enjoyed both the idea and the plot. I liked seeing that familiar detective-story atmosphere recreated in this setting.

  - title: "The Book Thief"
    author: "Markus Zusak"
    note: |
      A story about a young book thief in Nazi Germany that I wanted to like more than I did, but it felt like a drag for me. Personally, Jojo Rabbit worked better at showing that era through a child's perspective.

  - title: "The Stationery Shop of Tehran"
    author: "Marjan Kamali"
    note: |
      A lovely, beautiful story about love against the political upheaval of 1953 Tehran. I found it deeply touching, and it's one of those books I remember with a lot of affection. So, so good.

  - title: "Shiva Trilogy"
    author: "Amish Tripathi"
    note: |
      I put off The Immortals of Meluha because I thought it would be too preachy or religious, then couldn't stop once I started. I went straight on to The Secret of the Nagas and The Oath of the Vayuputras, and every book was a page-turner. A really great read, though be warned that the ending is sad.

  - title: "The Metamorphosis"
    author: "Franz Kafka"
    note: |
      Eccentric as hell and super weird. I'm glad I read it once, although I don't particularly feel the need to go back to it.

  - title: "The Last Queen"
    author: "Chitra Banerjee Divakaruni"
    note: |
      I loved reading about Maharani Jindan Kaur's journey from a modest background to becoming queen and then ruling as regent for her son. What stayed with me was the treatment of women as ornaments or spoils of war, and how she fought the prejudice and expectations surrounding her to exercise power herself. Seeing that history through her perspective made it especially compelling.

  - title: "The Ambuja Story"
    author: "Narotam Sekhsaria"
    note: |
      The story of building Ambuja Cements from the ground up. What first got me interested was learning that they used ships to transport cement, which struck me as such a novel and visionary thing to do. I wanted to understand the thinking behind decisions like that and how they helped build the company.

  - title: "Harsh Realities: The Making of Marico"
    author: "Harsh Mariwala and Ram Charan"
    note: |
      A great account of Harsh Mariwala building Marico and the difficulty of pushing past family expectations to create something of his own. I loved the marketing ideas and the attention to building a culture that could support the business. It felt like a very real example of why people say culture eats strategy for breakfast.

  - title: "The CEO Factory"
    author: "Sudhir Sitapati"
    note: |
      I was fascinated by how HUL became a breeding ground for CEOs, and what it takes to create an environment that helps people grow into great leaders. Another book that made me think about how much a company's culture shapes the people who spend years working there.

  - title: "The Toyota Way"
    author: "Jeffrey K. Liker"
    note: |
      One of the books that made me. Reading about Kanban and Toyota's approach to processes had a real influence on how I think about work, and why getting the process right matters so much. A book I associate with the way I learned to approach problems.

  - title: "Heart Lamp"
    author: "Banu Mushtaq; translated by Deepa Bhasthi"
    note: |
      A good collection of stories, some of which are genuinely heartbreaking. I found those harder to move on from than I expected.

  - title: "The Curse of Muziris"
    author: "Hamish Morjaria"
    note: |
      A Dan Brown-ish thriller in an Indian setting that fell flat for me towards the end. After all the build-up, the discovery didn't seem consequential enough to justify it, which took a lot of the excitement out of the finish.

  - title: "The Fault in Our Stars"
    author: "John Green"
    note: |
      A favourite. Judge me, go ahead. I've never seen the movie, but the book felt real and grounded to me in its portrayal of young people living with terminal illness. The love in it felt all the more affecting because of how little time they might have, and that sense of impermanence really stayed with me.

  - title: "Employees First, Customers Second"
    author: "Vineet Nayar"
    note: |
      I picked this up because I wanted to understand how to build great products, and was interested in what putting employees first might mean for the business around them.

  - title: "Illuminae"
    author: "Amie Kaufman and Jay Kristoff"
    note: |
      Borrowed this from a friend, and it felt like a big sci-fi movie told through an unusual mix of documents, visuals and other bits of storytelling. I wasn't a huge fan myself, but if you like sci-fi and want an accessible read that does something different with the format, give it a go.

  - title: "The Startup of You"
    author: "Reid Hoffman and Ben Casnocha"
    note: |
      Still only partially read. My former Product Manager gave this to me, which makes that admission a little more embarrassing. 😭

  - title: "The Secret of the Lost Necklace"
    author: "Enid Blyton"
    note: |
      My sister gave me this in seventh grade, and I still remember the childhood adventure and the necklace at the grandmother's house. It belongs to that period when stories like these made me desperately want to go to boarding school, presumably because I assumed there would be a mystery waiting for me every term.

  - title: "The Forest of Enchantments"
    author: "Chitra Banerjee Divakaruni"
    note: |
      Much like The Palace of Illusions, this made a familiar epic feel deeply personal, this time telling the Ramayana from Sita's perspective. So, so good. I also happened to meet the author at Bookworm in Bangalore without knowing who she was, and called her aunty. An excellent literary moment for me.

  - title: "Mahabharata"
    author: "C. Rajagopalachari"
    note: |
      A very balanced, concisely written retelling by India's last Governor-General. I really appreciated how clearly it brought together such a sprawling story.

  - title: "Deadly Skies"
    author: "Jim Eldridge"
    note: |
      Such a good, absorbing read from a fighter pilot's point of view. The writing kept me turning pages, and I remember feeling as though I was in the cockpit alongside him.

  - title: "Kane and Abel"
    author: "Jeffrey Archer"
    note: |
      At one point I was convinced this was the best book ever. The storytelling, the rivalry, the business feud, the romance, the money; I was completely caught up in all of it. Everything about it worked for me.
