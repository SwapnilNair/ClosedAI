I keep wondering how many ideas I have quietly abandoned because I had already decided that building them would be too much work. Usually there wasn't a particularly rigorous assessment involved; I would think about the unfamiliar tools, the weekends it might eat up, and the likelihood of getting stuck somewhere boring, and that was enough to send the idea back to wherever it had come from.

This seemed sensible for a long time. Now that AI is closing some of the distance between thinking of something and getting a working version in front of me, I suspect I need to revisit quite a few of those decisions. My estimate of what I can build appears to be updating considerably more slowly than the tools I use to build it.

I've been thinking of this as imagineering. [Disney already has an Imagineering division](https://sites.disney.com/waltdisneyimagineering/), so, no, not that one, although turning things somebody dreamt up into experiences people can actually walk into is close enough to what I mean. I would also quite like access to their animatronics budget.

In April 2020, Marc Andreessen published [It's Time to Build](https://a16z.com/its-time-to-build/), calling for much greater ambition in what society produces, from housing and manufacturing to education and healthcare. It's an essay worth returning to when thinking about the startup ambitions of the 2020s, especially because the things it asks for extend well beyond software. I like the impatience in it, the refusal to accept that we should keep tolerating problems we have the means to work on.

I want to carry that invitation a little further. As more of the work involved in trying an idea becomes accessible, I think we should spend some serious time imagining what we would actually like to exist, including the things we have trained ourselves to consider a bit ridiculous.

A recent attempt at this was [asking AI to help clean up my television](/blog/android-tv-exorcism), after discovering [CleanYourTV](https://github.com/Gathin23/cleanyourtv). The project and the work behind it belong to its creators; I found it, realised I could do this on my own TV, and tried it. I had spent ages treating the TV's software as a fixed part of the appliance, and then over the course of an evening I was changing its launcher, its screensaver and how I shared photos with it. Finding out that somebody had already made this possible was enough to get me going.

Then there was OfficeParty, which started because we couldn't find a speaker. We did have six Macs, and at some point wondered whether we could get all of them playing music together. It initially sounded like the sort of thing that would involve an unreasonable amount of effort, until we remembered how Google Meet manages audio across laptops and microphones in a conference room. That gave us enough confidence to try synchronising the machines ourselves, and by the end of the evening we had built OfficeParty, turning six Macs into one synchronised music system.

I don't think we would have seriously considered doing that without the Google Meet connection. Once it occurred to us, there was suddenly something to investigate, and getting a working version together in an evening made me wonder how many other ideas I had dismissed without even looking into them. Apparently being unable to find a speaker was all the encouragement we needed.

I suspect there are much more consequential versions of that sitting around us. A teacher might have a wonderful idea for explaining electricity through a little world students can build and break, but assume that making it would require a game studio. Somebody organising care for an elderly parent might be able to describe exactly how appointments, instructions and updates ought to fit together, having spent months dealing with the gaps themselves. The person who has spent ten years processing municipal applications probably has some fairly specific thoughts about how that experience could work (Not [BBMP](https://bbmp.gov.in/home) though, those guys suck).

I would love to see more of those people getting far enough with their ideas that somebody else can try them. A prototype gives the next conversation something concrete to work with, and the people using it will almost certainly suggest possibilities its creator hadn't considered. Being able to repeat that process cheaply makes it reasonable to explore ideas that would previously have been difficult to justify spending time on.

Of course, the hard parts continue well beyond a convincing demo. A tool used in a school or a care setting has to earn trust, handle mistakes and work for people whose circumstances its builder may never have encountered. Software also doesn't conjure hospital beds, electricity or planning permission into existence. Knowing a field deeply remains enormously useful, particularly when deciding whether the thing you have built is any good. I just think more people can now afford to get involved in that process, and bring much larger ambitions with them.

There is a habit I want to get better at myself: conjuring up moonshot ideas, the sort that sound slightly ridiculous when you first say them out loud but could have an enormous impact if they worked. I think about [AlphaFold](https://deepmind.google/science/alphafold/), and what making protein structure predictions widely available has opened up for researchers, or browse [the projects at X](https://x.company/projects/) and wonder why I so rarely let myself think at that scale. I would like to spend more time imagining things that would substantially change what people can do, even when I have absolutely no idea how to get there yet.

Then I'd like to actually try building a version, however rough, just once. I might have no clue which parts will work, which assumptions are hopelessly wrong, or what knowledge I'm missing, but spending some time with an experiment would at least give me a feel for what could be possible. It could be a simulation, a tiny piece of a much larger system, or something that fails in an interesting enough way that I want to understand why. I routinely throw these ideas at [Akash Mandal](https://www.linkedin.com/in/akash-mandal/), my Chief Architect and a voice of reason and sanity, who has to deal with whatever I've decided might be possible that day :) Having someone to think them through with helps me figure out what I'm missing and where I could actually start. I'd much rather give myself that chance than reject it because I couldn't map the whole picture from the beginning.

I think there's room for a lot more of us to approach our ideas this way, with enough curiosity to follow them somewhere unfamiliar and enough ambition to choose problems that would matter enormously (for whatever tiny fraction of people, even if it is just one) if we made progress. The tools are giving us more opportunities to try, and I want to get better at imagining things worth trying them on. At the very least I might just get better at naming things. [Project Suncatcher](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-research/google-project-suncatcher-facts/) is such a fantastic name Google ! 

Spaceflight needed some extraordinary physics and engineering, along with people willing to believe that something so far beyond everyday life was even worth attempting. That willingness is what I keep coming back to as AI makes more of the technical work accessible: how much of the barrier to building something great now comes from what we allow ourselves to imagine? 


Just like the barrier to spaceflight wasn't just physics - it was faith in doing the seemingly impossible - the barrier to building great stuff isn't technical ability, it is imagination. The barriers to building are coming down, and it's now time for us to Imagineer our future.
